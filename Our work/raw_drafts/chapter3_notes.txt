METHODOLOGY
This chapter outlines the methodology and architectural design of the proposed multi-modal mobile cardiovascular sensing system. The pipeline integrates non-invasive sternal seismocardiography (SCG) with optical photoplethysmography (PPG) as an automatic fallback, followed by automated signal conditioning, statistical pulse rate variability (PRV) extraction, and machine learning-based risk classification. To make the design easy to present and replicate, the system is described through an architectural block diagram, an operational flowchart, algorithmic pseudocode, and a summary specification table.

System Design
Overview
The system follows a modular four-tier architecture, chosen so that the heavy lifting runs on consumer mobile hardware while the backend stays lightweight.
Each tier is decoupled, meaning a failure or degradation in one layer (for example, poor SCG quality) can be handled by the next tier without restarting the whole pipeline.
The four layers (described as a block diagram flow, left to right):
Tier 1 – Data Acquisition Layer
Primary sensor: tri-axial sternal MEMS accelerometer (±2 g) sampling at 100–200 Hz, capturing the dorso-ventral (Z-axis) SCG signal.
Secondary/fallback sensor: optical fingertip camera PPG at 30–60 FPS, activated automatically when SCG quality is insufficient.
Tier 2 – Signal Conditioning and Pre-processing Layer
Attenuates out-of-band physiological noise, baseline wander, and motion artifacts using zero-phase digital filtering.
Includes a signal quality check based on SNR and posture gating (supine/static verification).
Tier 3 – Feature Extraction Layer
Uses an adaptive threshold detector to locate aortic opening (AO) systolic peaks.
Derives continuous Peak-to-Peak Intervals (PPI) and computes time-domain and frequency-domain PRV metrics.
Duplicate signals and reduce noise for accurate heart rate output
Tier 4 – Analytical and In-Silico Simulation Layer
Feeds PRV feature vectors into a dual-target supervised classifier (autonomic stress and rhythm irregularity).
Drives a coupled nonlinear cardiac oscillator (Van der Pol) for dynamical rhythm stability analysis.

Pseudocode — Sternal SCG Conditioning and Pulse Rate Variability Extraction
Inputs: raw dorso-ventral IMU signal a_z[n], sampling frequency f_s ∈ [100, 200] Hz, window size W = 60 s.
Outputs: mean heart rate (HR), SDNN, RMSSD, and LF/HF ratio.
Initialize: filter order N = 4, cutoffs f_l = 0.5 Hz and f_h = 40 Hz, refractory period δ_ref = 0.35 s.
Step 1 – Signal Conditioning
Apply a zero-phase Butterworth bandpass filter: ã_z = FiltFilt(a_z, f_l, f_h, N, f_s).
Compute the signal envelope via Hilbert transform or moving RMS: E[n] = sqrt of the moving mean of ã_z² over K samples.
Step 2 – Adaptive Systolic Peak Detection
Compute a local sliding threshold: θ[n] = μ_E[n] + α·σ_E[n], with α ≈ 0.6 chosen empirically.
Identify candidate peaks where E[n] > θ[n] and E[n] is a local maximum (E[n] > E[n±1]).
Reject physiologically invalid peaks by enforcing a minimum spacing: |n_i − n_{i−1}| / f_s ≥ δ_ref (corresponds to a maximum of about 180 bpm).
Step 3 – Tachogram Construction and Metric Extraction
Compute peak-to-peak intervals: PPI_k = (n_{k+1} − n_k)/f_s × 1000 ms.
Filter outliers: retain only PPI_k within 333–1500 ms.
Compute time-domain PRV: HR = 60000/mean(PPI); SDNN = standard deviation of PPI; RMSSD = root mean square of successive PPI differences (ΔPPI).
Interpolate the PPI tachogram uniformly to 4 Hz using cubic splines.
Compute the Welch power spectral density via FFT of the resampled tachogram.
Integrate band power: LF = integral of PSD from 0.04 to 0.15 Hz; HF = integral from 0.15 to 0.40 Hz; Ratio = LF/HF.
Return: {HR, SDNN, RMSSD, LF/HF}.
Technical Configuration Summary (table content in bullet form):
Hardware – Mobile sensors: sternal 3-axis MEMS accelerometer (±2 g, 100–200 Hz); rear camera (30–60 FPS).
Conditioning – Digital filter: 4th-order zero-phase Butterworth bandpass (0.5–40 Hz); cubic-spline detrending.
Software – DSP and modeling: Python 3.9+ with NumPy, SciPy.signal, and scipy.integrate.solve_ivp.
Machine learning: Scikit-Learn and XGBoost, with features HR, SDNN, RMSSD, and LF/HF.
Dynamical model: coupled Van der Pol nonlinear oscillators simulating autonomic cardiac stress.
Validation – Clinical benchmarks: PhysioNet MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database and the WESAD Stress Dataset.
Evaluation metrics: MAE and RMSE for heart rate (target < 3 bpm); Macro-F1 for stress states.

Data
Collection setup: data is captured from the phone's sternal-mounted accelerometer as the primary modality, with camera PPG as the fallback modality when the accelerometer signal fails quality checks.
Acquisition window: each measurement session captures a 30–60 s buffer (the 60 s window is used as the default for PRV estimation), which is long enough for frequency-domain analysis but short enough for a responsive user experience.
Posture and activity gating: samples are only accepted when the subject is supine/static, since posture changes and motion corrupt the SCG morphology. This is enforced before filtering.
Fallback data path: if posture gating fails or the SCG SNR falls below 12 dB, the pipeline switches to the PPG stream and uses it for peak detection and PRV extraction instead.
Pre-processing and cleaning: baseline wander and out-of-band noise are removed by zero-phase bandpass filtering; the envelope is computed via Hilbert transform or moving RMS; physiologically implausible beats are removed by the refractory-period rule and the 333–1500 ms PPI bounds.
Outlier handling: PPI values outside the physiological range are discarded before computing time-domain metrics; the remaining tachogram is cubic-spline interpolated to a uniform 4 Hz grid for spectral analysis.
Reference/benchmark datasets: PhysioNet MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database for rhythm irregularity, and the WESAD Stress Dataset for stress-state classification, used to validate the pipeline against established ground truth.

Methods
Signal conditioning: a 4th-order zero-phase Butterworth bandpass filter (0.5–40 Hz) removes baseline drift and high-frequency noise without introducing phase distortion, which is important because peak timing directly determines the PRV values.
Envelope extraction: a Hilbert transform or moving-RMS envelope converts the oscillatory SCG into a smooth energy signal so that systolic events appear as prominent, separable peaks.
Adaptive threshold peak detection: instead of a fixed threshold, the detector uses a local sliding threshold θ[n] = μ_E[n] + α·σ_E[n]. This adapts to changing signal amplitude across subjects and recording conditions.
Physiological rejection rules: a refractory period of 0.35 s caps the detectable rate at about 180 bpm, and PPI bounds of 333–1500 ms remove ectopic or missed-beat artifacts.
Time-domain PRV features: mean heart rate, SDNN (overall variability), and RMSSD (short-term/beat-to-beat variability) are computed from the cleaned PPI series.
Frequency-domain PRV features: the tachogram is cubic-spline interpolated to 4 Hz, and Welch PSD is used to integrate LF (0.04–0.15 Hz) and HF (0.15–0.40 Hz) power, with LF/HF as the autonomic balance indicator.
Machine learning classification: PRV feature vectors (HR, SDNN, RMSSD, LF/HF) are fed into a dual-target supervised classifier — Random Forest and XGBoost — to predict autonomic stress state and rhythm irregularity.
In-silico simulation: a coupled Van der Pol nonlinear oscillator model is driven by the extracted features to analyze dynamical rhythm stability, providing a mechanistic complement to the statistical classifier.

Hyperparameter Configurations
Sampling frequency (f_s): 100–200 Hz for the IMU; 30–60 FPS for camera PPG.
Filter order: N = 4.
Bandpass cutoffs: f_l = 0.5 Hz, f_h = 40 Hz.
Envelope window size: K samples for the moving RMS (chosen relative to f_s).
Adaptive threshold coefficient: α ≈ 0.6 (set empirically).
Refractory period: δ_ref = 0.35 s (max ~180 bpm).
PPI validity range: 333 ms to 1500 ms.
Tachogram resampling rate: 4 Hz (cubic spline interpolation).
Frequency bands: LF = 0.04–0.15 Hz; HF = 0.15–0.40 Hz.
Analysis window length: W = 60 s (with 30–60 s acquisition buffers).
SNR acceptance threshold: 12 dB before SCG peak detection is allowed.
ML models: Random Forest and XGBoost, trained on the four-feature PRV vector.
Note on tuning: these values are the configured starting points; final ML hyperparameters (tree depth, estimators, learning rate) are to be finalized during experimental trials rather than locked in at design time.

Performance Evaluation
Heart rate accuracy: measured with Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and RMSE against reference heart rate, with a target of less than 3 bpm error.
Stress-state classification: evaluated using Macro-F1 to account for class imbalance across stress/autonomic states.
Validation datasets: PhysioNet MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database (rhythm irregularity benchmark) and WESAD Stress Dataset (stress-state benchmark).
Comparison strategy: SCG-derived PRV is compared against the PPG fallback path and against the reference datasets to confirm that the primary modality meets accuracy targets and that the fallback degrades gracefully.
Robustness checks: performance is assessed under posture-gating failures and low-SNR conditions to verify that fallback triggering behaves as intended in the flowchart.
Simulation validation: the Van der Pol model output is compared qualitatively and quantitatively against observed PRV dynamics to confirm that the simulated rhythm stability trends match the real extracted features.

1. System Overview (the big picture)
The system has 4 layers that run one after another, like an assembly line.
Each layer works on its own, so if one part has a problem (like a bad chest signal), the system can switch to a backup without restarting.
Most of the work runs on the phone itself.
2. The 4 Layers [STEPS/PROCESS]
Layer 1: Collect the signal. The phone sits on the chest, and the accelerometer picks up heartbeat vibrations (SCG). If that signal is bad, the system switches to the camera + flash on the fingertip (PPG) as a backup.
Layer 2: Clean the signal. It filters out noise and drift, then checks whether the signal is good enough to use.
Layer 3: Heartbeat Detection. It detects each beat, measures the time between beats, and calculates PRV (heart rate variability).
Layer 4: Cardiac Forecast. Machine learning predicts stress and irregular rhythm, and a heart model simulates how the rhythm behaves under stress.
3. Flowchart (how one measurement runs)
Press start, and the phone records 30 to 60 seconds.
Check 1: Is the person lying still? If not, switch to the camera backup.
Check 2: Is the chest signal clean enough (SNR ≥ 12 dB)? If not, switch to the camera backup.
Find the heartbeats, calculate PRV, estimate stress, and show the results.
These two checks are the only points where the system switches to the backup sensor.

4. Pseudocode (the step-by-step algorithm)
Step 1: Clean. Filter the chest signal to keep only heartbeat frequencies (0.5 to 40 Hz), then smooth it so each beat shows up as a clear bump.
Step 2: Find beats. The threshold for a valid beat adjusts automatically to each person’s signal strength. Beats that come too close together (faster than 180 bpm) are ignored as fake.
Step 3: Calculate results. Measure the time between beats and throw out impossible values. Then calculate heart rate, SDNN, RMSSD, and LF/HF.
Output: heart rate, SDNN, RMSSD, and LF/HF.
5. Tools and Setup
Hardware: only the phone’s accelerometer and rear camera.
Software: Python with NumPy and SciPy for signal processing and the heart model.
Machine learning: Scikit-Learn and XGBoost.
Testing data: public datasets (MIT-BIH and WESAD).
Success targets: heart rate within 3 bpm of the true value, plus a good F1 score for stress detection.
6. Data
The chest accelerometer is the main sensor, and the camera is the backup.
Each recording is 60 seconds, long enough for the frequency analysis but still quick for the user.
Data is only accepted when the person is lying still, because movement ruins the chest signal.
Noise and fake beats are removed before any calculation.
Public datasets give us “correct answers” to check our results against.
7. Methods (why we chose each technique)
Zero-phase filter: removes noise without shifting beat timing, which matters because PRV depends on exact timing.
Envelope: turns the wavy signal into clear peaks, one per heartbeat.
Adaptive threshold: works for different people and conditions instead of using one fixed value.
Rejection rules: remove beats that are physically impossible.
PRV features: SDNN shows overall variability, RMSSD shows beat-to-beat changes, and LF/HF shows the stress balance.
Machine learning: Random Forest and XGBoost predict stress and irregular rhythm.
Simulation: the Van der Pol heart model shows why the rhythm changes, while the ML only predicts what will happen.
8. Settings (Hyperparameters)
These are the starting values for each step, such as the filter range, the 180 bpm limit, the 60 s window, and the 12 dB quality check.
The ML model settings will be tuned during testing, not fixed now.
